Abstract

Search content loses ranking performance over time — a phenomenon commonly called content decay — yet most editorial teams rely on manual audits or simple time-based heuristics to decide which pages to refresh. This study asks: can a supervised machine learning model, trained on aggregated production search signals, predict which pages are experiencing declining trend direction before human editors flag them? We applied a Random Forest classifier to a page-level dataset of 30,000 records aggregated from approximately 79 million rows of real production impression and click data across multiple content verticals. After engineering temporal and engagement features and conducting a rigorous validation audit — identifying and removing two distinct sources of label leakage, including a column that directly encoded the target label — the clean model achieved an observed ROC-AUC of 0.79 and a Precision@500 of 0.52 on a grouped-by-client holdout split, representing an estimated 37% lift over a rule-based frequency baseline evaluated on the same split. These results are directional and intended for decision-support only; they suggest that temporal engagement features carry meaningful predictive signal for refresh prioritisation, but the model should not be treated as production-ready without further validation against live editorial outcomes.

Section 1

Introduction & Problem Statement

Search engine optimisation (SEO) practitioners face a recurring challenge: content that ranked well when first published steadily loses search visibility as competitors publish fresher material, user intent shifts, and algorithmic weighting evolves. This degradation is rarely sudden — it manifests as a slow erosion of impressions and clicks over weeks or months, often invisible until significant ranking positions have already been lost.

Traditional responses to content decay rely on two approaches: periodic blanket audits (review everything every six months) or reactive triage (fix pages only after traffic drops become obvious). Both are inefficient. Blanket audits waste editorial capacity on pages that are performing fine; reactive triage means the opportunity cost of lost traffic has already accumulated.

A predictive model that scores pages by their likelihood of benefit from a refresh would change this workflow: editors could prioritise the top-N highest-risk pages, acting before decay becomes severe and directing effort where the signal is strongest.

The FlyRank Context

This study is the capstone of Lane 2: Content Refresh / Opportunity Scoring of the FlyRank ML Internship, and it addresses a real operational challenge that FlyRank faces as a content SEO platform: how do you scale refresh prioritisation across dozens of client accounts and tens of thousands of pages without overwhelming editorial teams?

FlyRank's content teams needed a smarter triage signal than blanket time-based rules — but building one required transforming raw production search telemetry into a clean, labelled, ML-ready dataset. That transformation, and the predictive model on top of it, is what this lane produced: an opportunity-scoring system that ranks pages by their likelihood of needing a refresh, grounded in real engagement signals rather than editorial intuition alone.

Research Question

Can a supervised machine learning model — trained on aggregated production search impression and engagement signals — predict which pages are currently on a declining trend, with better precision than a rule-based baseline, when evaluated on a client-grouped holdout?

Scope note: This study does not claim to predict future ranking loss. It predicts whether a page is currently labelled as declining (based on observed trend direction in the dataset). The operational value is in prioritisation — surfacing the right pages for review — not in forecasting absolute traffic numbers.

Exclusions & Filtering

The following records were excluded before modelling:

  • Pages with fewer than 50 impressions in the 90-day window — insufficient signal for reliable CTR estimation, causing label collapse when using median CTR as a threshold.
  • Records with null values in key feature columns after the aggregation step.
  • Raw query-level rows — all analysis operates at the page level exclusively.
Public-safety note: No client names, raw URLs, search queries, or any personally identifiable information appears in this study. All analysis uses the anonymised, aggregated dataset only.
Section 3

Methodology

3.1 Label Definition

The binary target label is_refresh_opportunity is derived from the trend_direction column in the dataset:

Label rule: is_refresh_opportunity = 1 if trend_direction == 'down', else 0.

This label is a proxy for editorial need, not a ground-truth measurement. Pages with a downward trend direction are treated as refresh candidates; all others are treated as non-candidates. The positive class rate is approximately 36% across the full dataset. An additional CTR threshold (40th percentile, applied to pages with ≥50 impressions) was used to avoid label collapse in low-impression strata.

3.2 Feature Engineering

Feature construction focused on signals that would be available at inference time without requiring future data. The final clean feature set (post-leakage-audit) includes:

  • Temporal: days_since_last_update — primary staleness signal
  • Volume: impressions_90d, clicks_90d
  • Efficiency: click_through_rate, position_avg
  • Content: word_count, content_length
  • Derived gap: CTR gap relative to cohort median (clipped to avoid division by zero)

No feature normalisation was applied before the Random Forest, which is invariant to monotonic feature transformations. CTR gap computation used max(float(ctr_gap_raw.max()), 1e-9) to handle scalar clipping correctly.

3.3 Baseline

The rule-based baseline flags a page as a refresh opportunity if both conditions are met:

  • Impressions in the 90-day window fall below the median for that client cohort.
  • Days since last update exceed 180 (six months).

This represents a plausible editorial heuristic: stale pages with declining traffic are the most obvious refresh candidates. All model comparisons are made against this baseline on the same validation split.

3.4 Leakage Audit

Two sources of label leakage were identified and removed before final model training:

FeatureLeakage TypeWhy It LeaksResolution
trend_pct Level 1 — Direct Numerically encodes trend_direction; near-perfect split on the label Removed entirely
impression_momentum
(last_30d / prev_30d)		 Level 2 — Indirect Ratio of recent to prior impressions is a near-perfect proxy for trend direction Removed entirely
Leakage impact: With both leaky features present, the model achieved an inflated ROC-AUC of ~0.97 on a random split. After removal and using a grouped-by-client split, AUC dropped to 0.79 — a significant and honest correction that reveals the true generalisation gap.

3.5 Model

A Random Forest classifier (scikit-learn) was selected for its robustness to feature scale, interpretable feature importances, and strong out-of-box performance on tabular data. Hyperparameters used:

HyperparameterValue
n_estimators100

No hyperparameter tuning was performed; the focus was on split strategy and leakage control rather than optimisation.

3.6 Validation Design

Three split strategies were compared to characterise the gap between optimistic and realistic performance estimates:

Split StrategyTrain / TestRationale
Random split 80% / 20% random rows Industry default; inflated because same-client rows appear in both sets
Grouped-by-client 26 clients train / 6 clients test Primary evaluation split — no client appears in both train and test
Time-aware Older content trains; fresher content tests Simulates deployment: model trained on past, tested on newer data

Section 5

Limitations & Honest Framing

The following limitations should be understood before interpreting or acting on these results:

5.1 Label is a Proxy, Not Ground Truth

The target label (trend_direction == 'down') reflects observed impression trend, not whether a refresh would actually improve performance. A page can be declining for reasons outside editorial control (seasonality, competitor cannibalism, algorithm shifts). Conversely, some stale pages may not need a refresh at all. No editorial outcome data was available to validate whether model-flagged pages actually benefited from refresh.

5.2 Aggregated Dataset Limits Feature Richness

The 30,000-row page-level dataset is an aggregation of 79 million raw rows. Fine-grained temporal patterns (week-over-week cadence, intra-day signals) are not available in this form. Future work with time-series features could improve predictive power.

5.3 Grouped Split May Still Underestimate Deployment Gap

While the grouped-by-client split prevents same-client leakage, it does not simulate deployment on entirely new domains or verticals. A content vertical absent from the training set may exhibit very different feature distributions, causing degraded performance in production.

5.4 No A/B Test Validation

These results are observational. There is no randomised experiment comparing editorial outcomes for pages the model flagged versus a control group. The 37% precision lift over the rule-based baseline is a ranking precision improvement, not a measured improvement in actual content performance.

5.5 Model Drift

The model has no self-updating mechanism. As search landscapes shift, the feature distributions may drift away from the training distribution. Periodic retraining would be required for any production deployment.

Recommended framing for any stakeholder communication: "This model provides a prioritisation signal — a smarter way to sort the refresh queue — not a guaranteed predictor of which pages will recover with refreshing."
Section 6

Ranked Recommendations

Based on findings from this study, the following actions are recommended in priority order:

  • 1

    Use the model for triage, not final decisions

    Deploy predictions as a prioritised queue for editorial review, not as an automated "refresh this page" trigger. The model surfaces candidates; human editors make the call.

  • 2

    Target consensus pages first (model + baseline agreement)

    Pages flagged by both the Random Forest model and the rule-based baseline are the highest-confidence candidates. Prioritise these as a high-precision starting point before moving to model-only flags.

  • 3

    Prioritise high-impression declining pages for maximum ROI

    Among model-flagged pages, sort by impressions_90d descending. A refresh on a page with 50,000 impressions at risk recovers far more traffic than the same effort on a 500-impression page.

  • 4

    Retrain with time-aware split before any production deployment

    The time-aware split (train on older content, test on fresher) is the most deployment-realistic scenario. Retraining on a full temporal split and validating against the most recent data window is necessary before treating any predictions as production signals.

  • 5

    Add editorial quality signals to future feature sets

    The current feature set is entirely search-telemetry based. Incorporating content signals — reading level, topic freshness, internal link depth, SERP feature coverage — could substantially improve label fidelity and predictive power.

  • 6

    Track post-refresh outcomes to build ground truth

    The single most valuable investment for improving future models is logging which flagged pages were refreshed and whether their impressions and CTR recovered within 60–90 days. This converts a proxy label into real editorial outcome data.
